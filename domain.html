The CITOS research story

This page is the group account of the project. Component methods, figures, and results stay on the Research pages.

Project overview

What CITOS is

CITOS, the Comprehensive Intelligent Transport Observation System, is an AI-driven framework for real-time driver behaviour analysis, vehicle monitoring, and road safety. It is a final-year research project at the SLIIT Faculty of Computing.

The work treats driver monitoring as more than one detector. A camera view of the driver, a view of the road, vehicle motion, and an explanation of traffic law answer different questions. CITOS is the frame that holds those questions together. It is not presented here as a single deployed product, and it is not presented as one experiment that has already validated every sensor at once.

Driver inattention and fatigue

Existing approaches
Reviews of driver inattention monitoring find that hybrid measures reduce false alarms compared with any single physical or driving-performance measure (Dong et al., 2011). Heart-rate variability is an established drowsiness indicator (Vicente et al., 2016; Fujiwara et al., 2019).
Limitations
Wearable sensing varies between people (Chowdhury et al., 2018). On this project, physiological labels are a proxy written from the same signals, not verified ground truth.
CITOS motivation
CITOS studies vision, physiology, and CAN as separate branches, and does not treat one sensor reading as a finished fatigue decision.

Driver distraction

Existing approaches
Vision systems classify secondary activities such as phone use, and surveys consolidate those methods into detection frameworks (Li et al., 2021; Kashevnik et al., 2021).
Limitations
Classifying an activity still does not show whether the driver is looking at the road. This paper does not yet report numbers for the distraction subsystem.
CITOS motivation
CITOS specifies activity classification together with head pose, traffic density, and route hazards.

Motion, pedals, and telematics

Existing approaches
Aggressive-event detection from vehicle motion has been compared across devices and places (Gatteschi et al., 2022). Foot gesture has been studied as a direct view of accelerator use (Tran et al., 2012).
Limitations
CAN and OBD-II capture the result of a manoeuvre rather than the control input, and a single speed threshold ignores the route. In CITOS the CAN labels describe driving risk, not fatigue.
CITOS motivation
The behaviour subsystem reads inertial motion directly. Pedal vision and predictive maintenance stay at the design stage.

Signals, context, and explainable coaching

Existing approaches
Low-visibility traffic-light detection has been pursued with YOLO-family models (Zhou et al., 2024). Adaptive retrieval often needs token probabilities, internal activations, or a fine-tuned confidence model (Jiang et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025).
Limitations
Those signals are unavailable from a commercial API, and a language model that answers from memory cannot show where a traffic-law claim came from.
CITOS motivation
The assessment subsystem answers from a Neo4j graph of operations and traffic law, and can retry according to why a query failed.

Research problem

One framework for several signals

The project-level problem is the gap between fragmented driver monitoring and a comprehensive intelligent transport observation framework. Safer driving needs more than one sensing source and more than one kind of analysis: who is driving, how they are driving, what the vehicle is doing, and which rule applies.

The research question at group level is how those complementary analyses can be organised so each one remains checkable, without pretending that separate datasets are one recording.

Research gap

Complementary work, still fragmented

Existing driver-monitoring and transport-safety approaches in this project focus on one problem or one sensing family at a time. A drowsiness frame, a cabin action, an IMU window, a dashcam violation, and a legal answer are useful on their own. They do not, by themselves, observe the driver and the trip together.

CITOS brings four research areas into one framework so those views can inform each other. That is the motivation. It is not a claim that the four areas have been validated end to end.

  1. 01

    Intelligent driver assessment and explainable coaching

    Detect traffic-light, crosswalk, and pedestrian events from the dashcam, decide a stop with GPS, score drivers by exposure rather than flat points, and answer traffic-law questions in English, Sinhala, Tamil, or Singlish only when the graph contains the evidence.

  2. 02

    Multimodal driver fatigue detection

    Study driver-facing vision for acted drowsiness, and keep physiological and CAN models on their own recordings. Do not treat a replayed file as a synchronized reading from the same driver at the same time.

  3. 03

    Route-aware behaviour and vehicle monitoring

    Classify driving style and passenger comfort from inertial windows. Pedal vision, route-specific baselines, and predictive maintenance are part of the design and are not yet implemented.

  4. 04

    Driver distraction detection

    Recognise distracting cabin activity and combine it with the road scene so the reported risk depends on both the driver and the road.

  1. Sensors

    Sensors and data

    The shared sensing layer is a driver-facing camera, a road-facing camera, GPS, an inertial unit, CAN or OBD-II, and wearable physiological signals. Pedal monitoring is specified by reusing the cockpit camera. It is not a separate synchronized recording, and the paper leaves pedal vision at the design stage.

  2. Data processing

    Processing

    Each component prepares its own input: landmarks and windowed vision features, IMU windows, GPS aligned to video time, or a replayed physiology or CAN sequence.

  3. Research components

    Individual research components

    Assessment, fatigue, behaviour, and distraction stay separate studies. They do not share one training table.

  4. AI / ML / DL models

    Models

    The published components use a landmark BiLSTM, a frame CNN baseline, a cabin CNN, YOLOv8m, Random Forest models, and a GraphRAG pipeline over Neo4j. Physiology and CAN use their own sequence models on separate files.

  5. Component outputs

    Component outputs

    Outputs include a vision drowsiness probability, a distraction class, a behaviour and comfort reading, a violation record, a driver score, and a grounded legal answer.

  6. Integration

    Integration

    The fatigue prototype can mix a live vision score with replayed physiology and CAN using fixed weights. That mix is an integration choice. It is not a joint experiment on one synchronized dataset.

  7. Presentation / alerts

    Dashboard, alerts, and explanations

    Component interfaces surface alerts, scores, and, in the assessment work, answers that cite the graph or abstain. This site does not report one system-level trial of the full stack.

System architecture

Four layers

The inference layer is where the four research areas sit. Detailed diagrams remain on the component pages.

Layer 1

Sensing layer

Driver-facing video, road-facing video, GPS, inertial measurements, CAN or OBD-II, and wearable signals. Integration is late: scores and decisions are combined, not raw features. No synchronized recording links all four subsystems.

Layer 2

Inference layer

Four complementary studies: intelligent driver assessment, driver fatigue detection, route-aware behaviour analytics, and driver distraction detection.

Layer 3

Integration layer

Component outputs can be shown together and, in the fatigue prototype, combined with stated weights. Cross-corpus fusion is not treated as proof that the sensors were recorded together.

Layer 4

Presentation layer

Dashboards, driver alerts, score explanations, and the GraphRAG answer. Detailed evidence stays on the research pages.

Overall research direction

Checkable components inside one framework

The direction of CITOS is to keep the four areas complementary and inspectable. Assessment explains violations and the law. Fatigue studies drowsiness without upgrading a replay into a live wearable. Behaviour reads the vehicle motion. Distraction reads the cabin against the road.

The limits below are part of that direction. They are stated so the group story does not run ahead of the component evidence.

Physiological labels are generated from the same variables later given to the model. They measure a fatigue proxy, not verified ground truth.

CAN labels describe driving risk rather than fatigue. The paper also notes that the scaler was fitted before the driver split.

The subsystems use different datasets and protocols. Their results are not a system-level comparison.

Pedal vision, route-aware contextual scoring, and predictive maintenance are designed but not implemented.

Distraction detection is specified, and its quantitative evaluation is not yet reported.

Cross-subsystem fusion is specified and not experimentally validated. No synchronized collection links all four subsystems, and no integrated system accuracy is reported.
